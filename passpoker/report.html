Pass Poker: which version plays best?

Playtest report from computer simulations, October 2026. Written by Claude for Julian and their brother.

Short answer: of the three versions you've played, Even split has slightly the most depth. But in all three, the passing and bidding barely decide who wins; the draft does. A small change to the draft, called Spoils below, makes the passing game matter a lot. It's now playable as a fourth option.

How this was tested

We can't play thousands of games ourselves, so I wrote computer players at different skill levels and had them play each other. Each level adds one skill:

  1. Random: does everything at random.
  2. Drafter: drafts sensibly, picking the card that best builds toward a strong final hand, but bids and passes at random.
  3. + Passing: also passes away its weakest cards and sorts overflows well.
  4. + Bidding: also bids low on strong hands and high on weak ones, and avoids sending both batches to the same opposing hand.
  5. + Denial: also drafts and passes to keep the opponent from completing their hand.

If adding a skill makes a player win clearly more than 50% against the level below it, that skill matters in that version. A game with depth has several skills that each pay off, so there's always more to get better at. The bots are simple, so these numbers are a floor: real players reading each other will find more.

What this says:

  • The draft is where the game is won. A player who drafts well beats a random drafter 96% of the time, in every version.
  • Bidding hardly matters. I tried several bidding styles: always low, always high, protect-the-strong-hand, and random. None beat random bidding by more than a few points. The one real edge is in Even split: don't send both batches into the same opposing hand, because the player who has to sort an overflow gets to pick the best 5 of up to 9 cards. That's worth about 4 points.
  • Why the passing phase is so weak: every card in both players' hands ends up in a draft pool, so passing doesn't keep cards away from anyone. Winning a matchup only means you pick first instead of second from 10 cards, and the second-best card is usually nearly as good.
  • Ranking: Even split is slightly deepest, mainly because the overflow rule adds a real choice. Split by your bids is close behind. Original is the shallowest: its swaps always balance, so there are never overflow decisions, and its passing edge is the smallest.

A fourth version: Spoils

The rule change: everything plays like Even split, except that in each matchup's draft, the winner picks first and third. The winner takes two cards, the loser one.

That means you can end the game with anywhere from 6 to 12 cards. The best 5 wins, and if those tie, you compare the leftover cards from highest down. Having more cards gives you more ways to make a strong hand, so every matchup is worth fighting for.

With Spoils, every skill pays off, including bidding, which was nearly irrelevant before. Bidding is the theme's signature mechanic, so it should matter. Spoils pairs best with Even split, the only passing rule where the bidding edge also shows up (61%).

The "vs random" number is about 95% in any real game, so it doesn't tell you much. The useful number is what each deeper skill is worth. Cribbage sits at about 60%, Spoils is at or a little above that, and the original versions are below it. One difference: a cribbage game is about 9 deals, and small edges add up over more deals. A Pass Poker game is 2 rounds, so any single game is more about the cards you're dealt, even when the skill is there. The skill shows up over many games.

Add-on: first-pick auction

This is an optional switch. Before each matchup's draft, both players secretly play one of their leftover bid cards, and the lower number picks first (a tie goes to the matchup winner). Lower wins, not higher, so it works as a comeback: the player who passed a lot (usually the one with weaker hands) keeps their low cards for the draft. With higher-wins, the matchup winner also won the auction 66% of the time. With lower-wins it was 36%.

Add-on: offer one extra (3rd pass)

This is another optional switch. On the 3rd pass, each hand offers one more card than it has to, and the receiver sends one back. It goes either to the hand it came from, or (Julian's twist) to the passer's other hand, a sneaky way to move a card between your own hands.

The bots can't judge the other-hand mode well. It's a bluffing decision: you offer a card you'd like moved, and the receiver has to guess whether you want it back. A bot that simply offered its most useful card lost a little, because the receiver often kept it. That's a real risk-and-reward call, which is good, but only playing it will show whether it's fun.

Where the depth is (so it doesn't get solved)

  • Bids are secret and simultaneous. There's no single best bid, because the right one depends on what the other person plays. That makes it a reading game, like rock-paper-scissors with stakes, and those don't get solved into one fixed strategy.
  • Bid cards run out. Each card is used once per round, so spending your 4s early leaves you without them in pass 3. That's planning across the whole round.
  • Round 2 is open information. Both players can see each other's kept cards, so in round 2 you can pass and draft to block what your opponent needs (the "denial" skill above).
  • Overflows are a weapon. Making your opponent sort an overflow helps them, and forcing yourself into one helps you. Neither of you controls it alone.

Risks and things to watch

  • Spoils may be too decisive. Between two equal bots, whoever wins more matchups wins the game 87% of the time (66% in Even split). That's good for skill, but a bad round 1 may feel hard to come back from. If it does, a milder version gives the extra pick only in the Best five of ten matchup.
  • Bots aren't people. These players don't bluff, read patterns, or adapt. Real play will have more depth than shown here. The comparison between versions is the useful part.

Recommendation

Play a few games of Spoils against each other. If it feels too swingy, try the milder version above. If you'd rather stay with what you've played, Even split is the deepest of the original three.
